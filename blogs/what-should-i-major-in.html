What Should I Major In?

Louis Newman

College Success Coach

Many students struggle to decide what to major in. It feels like an education-defining decision, and some students are overwhelmed by the options. Or maybe the major you thought you wanted isn’t working out as you had hoped—the classes aren’t interesting or you’re just not doing well.

The first thing to know when considering a major is that one of the most persistent misconceptions in higher education is that your college major functions as a career blueprint. This belief drives premature decisions, generates unnecessary anxiety, and leads students to close off possibilities before they have enough information to make genuinely informed choices.

The data tells a different story. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York consistently shows that fewer than 30% of college graduates work in fields directly related to their undergraduate major. A biology major becomes a financial analyst. An English graduate builds a career in software product management. Statistically, these aren't failures of planning; they're the norm. Employers value transferable skills: analytical reasoning, written communication, quantitative literacy, and collaborative problem-solving. These competencies develop across many fields and transfer across industries.

The major-equals-career assumption persists partly because a handful of professions, including nursing, engineering, and accounting, require specific technical credentials from the start. These exceptions get overgeneralized into a rule that doesn't apply to most career paths. A student interested in healthcare policy could reasonably get there through political science, economics, public health, or sociology. Many destinations have multiple routes.

Being undecided isn't a problem that needs urgent fixing. In fact, it’s often the most sensible and productive position insofar as it signals an openness to exploring a range of subjects and the possibility of discovering a new passion. Students entering college at 17 or 18 have limited exposure to professional environments, industries, and to what various careers actually look like day-to-day. Committing firmly to a major under those conditions risks relying on parental expectations or simple familiarity rather than genuinely investigating what you’re both interested in and good at. For this reason, many advisors and researchers now advocate for structured exploration during the first two years, using electives, internships, and informational interviews to build real evidence about your interests and strengths before declaring.

Allow yourself to get comfortable with the uncertainly of not knowing, especially at first, what subject(s) you want to major in. Students who use the exploratory period deliberately, noticing what genuinely engages them, seeking varied experiences, and treating coursework as information about themselves, are far better positioned to make a meaningful declaration when the time comes. Pressure to decide quickly often produces decisions that feel certain but rest on shallow foundations.
