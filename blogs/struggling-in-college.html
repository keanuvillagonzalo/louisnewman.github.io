College TransitionSeptember 23, 2026

My student did well in high school.
Why are they struggling in college?

Louis Newman
College Success Coach

The transition from high school to college is more than a change in location or course difficulty. It requires a fundamental shift in how you relate to learning itself, and students who don't make this shift consistently underperform relative to their actual ability.

High school does a lot of the cognitive work for students. Teachers monitor attendance, issue reminders about assignments, and build substantial structure around deadlines and expectations. A reasonably diligent student can succeed mostly by responding to external cues. College dismantles this structure almost entirely. According to the National Survey of Student Engagement, first-year students frequently report feeling blindsided not by the difficulty of the coursework, but by the absence of guidance they previously took for granted. The issue isn't intellectual capacity; it's an unexamined reliance on external accountability systems.

The shift college demands is a move from reactive to proactive learning. High school rewards compliance; college rewards responsibility, initiative, and consistency. Students who wait to be told what matters, when to study, or how deeply to engage will find that college faculty largely assume adult learners bring their own motivation. Professors cover material in lectures that often represents only a fraction of what students need to master independently through reading, synthesis, and practice.

This has real consequences. Students who treat college as an advanced version of high school tend to underutilize office hours and mismanage unstructured time, which makes up the majority of a college schedule. They also approach exams as single events to cram for rather than as ongoing assessments of cumulative understanding. Research published in the Journal of College Student Retention identifies metacognition, a student's awareness of their own learning process, as one of the strongest predictors of academic persistence and GPA.

The practical implication is clear: examining your assumptions about how learning is supposed to feel and work pays real dividends. That means building personal accountability systems, engaging with material between classes rather than only before exams, and treating confusion as a signal to seek help rather than a reason to disengage.

College operates on different assumptions from high school. Students who internalize that distinction early gain a genuine advantage over those who discover it through repeated difficulty.
