How Do I Distinguish Good College Advice from Bad?

Louis Newman

College Success Coach

Most high school students receive plenty of college advice, but how much of it is really useful?

Well-meaning counselors, parents, and websites hand out long checklists: shadow a professional, take AP courses, build your extracurricular profile, polish your essay. What they rarely do is separate the advice that actually builds college readiness from the advice that simply shows effort.

The result is predictable: students who work hard throughout junior and senior year still arrive on campus unprepared for what college actually demands.

The real question isn't how much preparation you should do. It's the kinds of preparation that translate into the skills and confidence that help students thrive rather than spend their freshman year scrambling to catch up. Research on academic transitions, combined with what college students consistently say looking back, reveals a meaningful gap between conventional preparation wisdom and what actually works. That gap is where genuinely useful guidance begins.

There is no “cookie cutter” advice about how to prepare for college that applies to everyone; each student arrives with different strengths and challenges. But there are some common themes that are worth paying attention to.

  • Be ready to do more of your work independently and outside of class than you likely did in high school.
  • Be prepared to manage your own calendar and take responsibility for getting assignments in on time, which often means working on a large project gradually and consistently over weeks.
  • Keep in mind that you’re now being asked to do more than absorb information from your teachers and demonstrate that you learned it. You are now being asked to think critically about everything you learn, which means asking probing questions about its origins, its validity, and its implications (among other things).
  • You are likely to need help at some point in your college career. The sooner you learn to swallow your pride and admit you can’t necessarily do it alone, the more likely that you’ll get the help you need before it’s too late.

And here’s a pro tip: when you’re reading college advice, consider its source—the expertise and experience of the author. There’s lots of advice from people, all of whom no doubt are well-meaning, but only some of whom really know what they’re talking about. Parents of students, former college students, general life coaches—all these people have perspectives and are often eager to share their experience and advice.

But it is better to look for advice or college coaching from people who have experience within academic institutions and a track record of supporting college students. Ideally, you want to find someone who has professionally advised or coached college students over many years and worked with many hundreds of students on all kinds of issues.

Experience Matters

With no formal “seal of approval” or “accreditation” to distinguish among them, anyone can offer themselves as an expert on college success. But the advice you get from people who have spent their careers working with students in college are best positioned to help your student through their college journey.
