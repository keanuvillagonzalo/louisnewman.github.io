The prestige trap: Why Rankings Mislead More Than They Inform

Louis Newman

College Success Coach

The idea that you should attend the most prestigious school you got into is so widely accepted that most families never stop to question it. This logic has a serious flaw: prestige measures an institution's reputation, not your individual outcomes.

The US News and World Report rankings, which drive many college decisions, assess factors like peer assessment scores, alumni giving rates, and selectivity. None of these directly measures what you'll learn, earn, or become. Two studies by economists Stacy Dale and Alan Krueger found that students accepted to elite schools who chose to attend less selective institutions earned salaries comparable to those of peers who enrolled at those elite schools. Ambition and capability drove earnings, not the school's brand.

Rankings also ignore fit entirely. Consider a pre-med student at a highly ranked research university competing in enormous introductory courses graded on a brutal curve, damaging the GPA they need for medical school. That same student at a strong regional university might earn a higher GPA, build real faculty relationships, and access research opportunities that were out of reach in a larger, more competitive environment. The prestigious school's name means little if it narrows your path to your actual goal.

The financial reality compounds this further. Average student debt at private universities now exceeds $35,000, and many graduates carry far more. When a highly ranked school offers minimal financial aid while a strong less prestigious university offers a merit scholarship, the lifetime cost difference can exceed $150,000. That money represents investment capital, career flexibility, and financial security that prestige simply cannot replace.

A better question than "which school has the highest ranking" is: which school offers the strongest program in my intended field, the most accessible faculty, a financial package that won't leave me buried in debt, and an environment where I'm likely to thrive?

Rankings measure institutional reputation. You'll live an individual experience. That distinction is one of the most important shifts you can make in how you approach college decisions.

Making the Right Choice

Determining which college to attend is a major decision. For most high school seniors, the most consequential decision they’ve likely ever made. The stakes seem enormous (which is generally not the case), and it’s hard to know what guidance to follow.

But the most important thing to focus on is: how well-suited am I to this environment? How certain am I that I will get the support I’ll need? And what do I need to do to succeed, no matter which choose I attend?

Sometimes what families need is not admissions-specific counseling but reassurance that they can ignore rankings and prestige and think instead about how their student can get a great education at the school they attend.
