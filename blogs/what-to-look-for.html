What Should We Look for in a College?

Louis Newman

College Success Coach

Most families evaluate colleges through a familiar lens: acceptance rates, rankings, campus aesthetics, and sticker price. These metrics reflect visibility, not necessarily value. Several features of undergraduate education have a much greater impact on student outcomes, yet rarely come up on campus tours or appear in comparison spreadsheets.

First-year seminar programs are one of the clearest differentiators. Schools that place incoming students in small, faculty-led seminars, typically capped at 15 to 20 students, help students make the critical shift from passive listener to active academic participant. The National Survey of Student Engagement consistently shows that students who experience these high-impact practices early in college demonstrate stronger retention and graduation rates. The question to ask isn't whether a school offers these seminars, but whether every student is required to take one. Schools that have required first-year seminars are telling you that they understand how important this experience is to long-term student success.

Study abroad accessibility deserves more scrutiny than the brochure version does. A university might advertise that 40% of students study internationally while quietly omitting that most programs require out-of-pocket costs well beyond standard financial aid. Ask specifically whether institutional aid applies to approved abroad programs and whether credits transfer without academic penalty. Study abroad programs are often among the most impactful educational experiences you have in college; be sure that you college makes it easy to take advantage of these opportunities.

Internship funding is a largely overlooked, but very consequential, factor in determining whether you get experiences that often open career paths after graduation. Students from middle-income households regularly turn down prestigious opportunities in policy, nonprofits, and the arts because they can't afford an unpaid summer in an expensive city. Many selective liberal arts colleges, Amherst, Williams, Pomona and Carleton, among them, maintain dedicated funds to cover living expenses during unpaid internships. This single feature can meaningfully expand the direction a graduate's career can take.

Undergraduate research access requires more than a quick look at the brochure. At large research universities, you want to ask whether undergraduates work meaningfully alongside faculty or primarily assist graduate students. Course-based undergraduate research experiences, where original inquiry is built into regular coursework, tend to distribute this opportunity more equitably than competitive lab placements. Also, be sure to explore whether the school offers funding for independent research projects under the supervision of a faculty or staff member.

Teaching quality is difficult to assess but is probably the most significant factor in the quality of the education you will receive in college. Schools with low student-to-faculty ratios generally give you more access to the faculty, which is ultimately what you’re paying for. (Beware, though of the numbers of courses taught by graduate teaching assistants or adjunct, part-time faculty who may be less experienced or have less investment in the institution.).

Ask for the percentage of introductory-level credit hours taught by tenure-track or tenured faculty. And ask what the university does to help early career faculty gain teaching skills and how they provide ongoing faculty development opportunities that allow teachers to hone their skills and experiment with new pedagogies. Institutions rarely volunteer this information, but if they decline to share it, you can regard that as a red flag.

Together, these indicators reveal how committed a school actually is to undergraduate success, rather than to burnish their credentials. Families who dig into this level of detail are far better positioned to find genuine educational value rather than marketed prestige.

Make a Strong Choice

A strong college career begins by making a strong choice of school, one based on metrics that measure student outcomes, impactful learning experiences, and resources to support faculty and students. After all, it’s an education you’re paying for.

The quality of that education is never predictable in advance, but there are factors that make outstanding educational experiences more likely. Those are the criteria that matter.
