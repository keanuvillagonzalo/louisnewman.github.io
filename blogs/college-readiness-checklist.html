Is My Kid Ready for College?
A Readiness Checklist for Parents

Louis Newman
College Success Coach

Most conversations about “college readiness” focus on test scores and GPAs. But the students who struggle most in their first year often aren't the ones who got a B in AP Chemistry: they're the ones who never learned how to manage their own time, ask for help, or recover from a setback without a parent stepping in.

After working with hundreds of families through the transition to college, I've found that academic readiness and life readiness are two different things, and most families only assess the first one, and even then, only partially.

Here's a checklist to help you evaluate both.

The academic side

  • Can your student read complex, unfamiliar material and (mostly) make sense of it on their own?
  • Do they know how to break a big assignment into a weekly plan, not just cram the night before?
  • Do they know how to manage their time effectively and especially how to juggle multiple responsibilities at once?
  • Do they have experience doing college-level work? Many high school students are able to get good grades by simply memorizing what they’re taught and “regurgitating” it accurately on tests. College courses will require high level analysis, synthesis, and evaluation of course material.
  • Have they ever gone to a teacher's office hours on their own, without being told to?
  • Does your student demonstrate intellectual curiosity, investigating issues or questions purely to expand their knowledge, even when this isn’t assigned or graded?
  • Does your student practice metacognition, that is, paying attention to the way they think and learn, noting what works well and where they can improve?

The life-skills side

  • Do they wake up on their own, or does someone else get them up?
  • Can they do their own laundry, budget a week of groceries, or handle a minor conflict with a roommate-type person without an adult intervening?
  • When something goes wrong (a bad grade, a fight with a friend, a missed deadline) do they problem-solve, or do they expect a parent to fix it?
  • Have they ever managed their own schedule (appointments, part-time job, commitments) without reminders?
  • Can they advocate for themselves (asking a professor for an extension, calling their insurance company, requesting accommodations) without a parent making the call?
  • Do they know why they're going to college, beyond “it's what you do after high school”? Students with a reason tend to weather the hard weeks better than students without one.
  • Have you had an honest conversation about what happens when things get hard: who they'll call, what “struggling” looks like before it becomes a crisis, and what your role will and won't be once they're there?

What to do if you checked “No” more than twice

A few gaps here are completely normal. No 17- or 18-year-old is fully independent yet, and that's not a failure on anyone's part. But if you're seeing a pattern, especially on the life-skills side, it's worth addressing before your student leaves, not after they're already struggling on campus.

That's the gap this checklist is really pointing at. Academic prep gets plenty of attention: tutors, test prep, admissions counselors. The life-skills and resilience side is where families are usually on their own, and it's where I focus my work.
